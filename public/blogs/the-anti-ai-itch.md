## Introduction

In early November 2026, itch.io celebrated removing several terabytes of what it called AI-generated spam. For the segment of the internet that treats the mere presence of generative AI as an offense, this was apparently cause for applause.

I see something very different. Itch was a platform entrusted with independent developers' work publicly boasting about the scale of its enforcement, shortly after admitting that its own moderation and indexing systems had incorrectly restricted legitimate projects on an enormous scale. Developers' livelihoods are simply not an acceptable price for a platform's public-relations victory.

Nobody is forced to download a game because it exists on itch.io. Nobody is forced to purchase it, play it, or recommend it. If you dislike generative AI, you can avoid games that disclose its use (Or indeed perfectly normal games that you make conspiracy theories about). But a developer whose work is wrongly hidden or removed cannot simply opt out of the consequences. They may lose a launch window, an audience, sales, or a rare opportunity that will never come around again.

Removing actual spam is reasonable, Sacrificing legitimate developers to do it is not.

## The Purge and the Applause

Itch.io's November announcement described the removal of several terabytes of AI-related spam amid a surge in new project submissions. There is a genuine problem hiding inside that headline: storefronts can be overwhelmed by automated submissions, repetitive listings, and malicious uploads. Those deserve attention.

But the announcement's emphasis on *terabytes removed* makes for a better social-media victory lap than it does a demonstration of responsible moderation. It tells us nothing about the number of legitimate projects affected, the standards applied, or the availability of meaningful recourse.

The political and cultural incentives here are obvious. Anti-AI sentiment is loud online. Publicly announcing that you have wiped out mountains of “AI slop” is an easy way to win applause from that audience. While I cannot establish that chasing applause was itch.io's internal motive. But I can absolutely criticize a public message that celebrates enforcement volume without showing the corresponding safeguards for developers.

And there is no evidence that the people most vocal on social media represent anything close to a majority of itch.io's users. The size of that constituency should not be invented to justify either side of this debate. The more important point is that even if a majority wanted aggressive removal, that would not excuse wrongful action against innocent developers.

## Itch.io Knew Its Systems Were Making Mistakes

On October 19, 2026, itch.io founder Leaf Corcoran published an update describing serious problems in project indexing. The platform acknowledged false positives from automated heuristics and classifiers, as well as bugs and review backlogs. It reported releasing approximately **215,000 previously blocked project pages** for indexing in a single week.

Let that sink in. Legitimate creators had been caught by an enforcement and discovery apparatus that itch.io itself admitted was producing incorrect outcomes. Those October cases concerned indexing restrictions, not necessarily deleted projects. Nor does that admission prove that the identical system was used to select November's deletions. But it destroys any basis for blind confidence that itch.io's automated decisions are inherently reliable.

Just weeks after acknowledging this problem, itch.io was celebrating another sweeping moderation action. The burden should have been on the platform to explain exactly how it protected legitimate developers from false positives, what human review occurred, and how mistakes could be reversed. Instead, the headline number was storage volume.

When you already know your moderation infrastructure has failed legitimate creators, your first priority should be preventing more collateral damage—not congratulating yourself on how much you removed.

## A Game's Existence Does Not Harm You

This is the part of the anti-AI argument I find especially difficult to take seriously.

A game being available for download does not compel you to play it. It does not force you to buy it. It does not prevent you from choosing something else. If a game's use of generative AI offends your personal or artistic principles, don't support it. To me this is no different from claiming that somebody else having their own religion somehow infringes on your right to have another religion, It's the same preposterous argument For people that don't like something and Can't point to a concrete reason that it needs to go.

There can be genuine external harms associated with particular projects—fraud, infringement, impersonation, malware, deceptive marketing—and those warrant enforcement on their own merits. A flood of spam can also harm discovery by drowning out legitimate work. But those harms need to be demonstrated, not presumed from the mere existence of a game made with a disliked tool.

Itch.io can (And amusingly enough already does) provide disclosure and filtering so people who want to avoid AI-assisted work can do so. That's a choice made by the consumer. Removing or mistakenly suppressing a legitimate developer's work is a choice imposed by the platform on someone else.

These are not remotely equivalent consequences.

## The Cost to Indie Developers Is Not Reversible

Independent development is not a level playing field. Many creators have no publisher, marketing department, advertising budget, or established audience. They have one release, one game jam, one burst of attention, one creator who might stream their project, or one chance to appear in a search result at exactly the right time.

Visibility matters disproportionately to these developers. If a project is wrongly excluded from discovery during launch week, reinstating it a month (or even a day) later does not restore the people who never found it and those people matter overwhelmingly. If a release is removed during a wave of attention, the audience may already have moved on. If a buyer or publisher encounters a dead page, they may never return. Even reversible actions can cause irreversible opportunity costs.

A platform cannot reliably compensate for a lost recommendation, an abandoned collaboration, a missed press opportunity, or the momentum of a launch that never happened. These are not abstract inconveniences. For a small developer, they can determine whether the next project gets made at all.

And for what? So somebody who never intended to play the game can enjoy knowing it is no longer listed? Protecting someone from seeing a game they dislike is not more important than protecting a legitimate creator from losing the opportunity to reach an audience because the later is Itch's actual job as a platform.

## Spam Is Behavior, Not a Development Tool

A game is not spam because its developer used an LLM, generated a texture, or asked a model to help debug a difficult problem. Spam is about abusive submission patterns, deception, repetition, and the manipulation of a platform's systems. A human can mass-upload thousands of near-identical projects. A developer who uses AI can spend years producing a carefully engineered and genuinely original game.

The tools alone cannot tell you which is which Even if we could reliably detect the tool.

This distinction matters because once a platform allows cultural hostility toward a technology to stand in for evidence of abuse, enforcement stops being about whether a project violates meaningful quality or safety standards. It becomes a referendum on how the developer worked.

Itch.io's published quality guidelines do address mass-produced and low-quality submissions, and its stated November objective was spam removal—not a universal prohibition on AI-assisted games. That distinction is important. But it also makes transparency essential: show that the projects removed were actually abusive, rather than expecting developers to trust a classification process whose broader ecosystem has already produced major false positives.

## Terabytes Are a Publicity Metric, Not an Accuracy Metric

Imagine two moderation operations:

| Metric | Operation A | Operation B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Data removed | 5 TB | 500 GB |
| Confirmed abusive projects removed | 500 | 5,000 |
| Legitimate projects wrongly affected | 100 | 5 |
| False-positive share | 16.7% | 0.1% |

*These are hypothetical figures, not itch.io statistics.*

Which operation would you celebrate? The one with the bigger number in the headline, or the one that removed more abuse while hurting fewer innocent developers?

Terabytes measure bytes. They do not measure moderation quality. A few oversized asset packs could outweigh thousands of complete games. The meaningful numbers would be projects reviewed, confirmed violations, false-positive rates, overturned decisions, and time to restoration.

If those figures aren't available, a triumphant announcement about storage removed should inspire skepticism, not applause.

## What Itch.io Owes Its Developers

A platform built around independent creators should treat mistaken enforcement as a serious failure, not acceptable collateral damage. At minimum, itch.io should explain the criteria and review procedures used in the November purge, disclose the number of affected projects, provide prompt appeals, and publish the number of decisions reversed.

Projects that present an immediate security threat may need immediate removal. But suspected low-quality or AI-generated content is not inherently an emergency. Where feasible, uncertain cases should receive human review before a destructive action (Or maybe, you know, talking to the developer first but what do I know?). And where a mistake has already occurred, restoration should include attention to lost indexing and discovery, not merely restoring a page and declaring the problem solved.

I would also like itch.io to answer a simple question: *hat false-positive rate does it consider acceptable when the consequences fall on independent developers?

---

## Conclusion

I am entirely in favor of removing genuine spam. Nobody benefits from a storefront drowning in duplicate uploads, malware, scams, or deceptive listings.

But legitimate developers should never be treated as expendable in the pursuit of cleaner search results or better social-media optics. It is especially unacceptable for a platform to trumpet the scale of its enforcement while leaving unanswered questions about safeguards, after recently acknowledging that its own systems had wrongly restricted an enormous number of legitimate projects.

The imbalance is staggering. Someone who dislikes an AI-assisted game can simply not play it. A developer wrongly suppressed by the storefront cannot simply recover the audience, revenue, momentum, or professional opportunities lost during that restriction.

Itch.io's first obligation should be to the creators who rely on it—not to whichever faction is loudest online this week.

A platform does not deserve applause for deleting terabytes of content until it can demonstrate that the people who paid the price were actually the people breaking the rules.
